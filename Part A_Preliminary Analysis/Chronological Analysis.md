## 2. Short Argument: When Did Ukraine Have Genuine Monetary Sovereignty?

Ukraine's monetary history shows that effective monetary sovereignty varied substantially over time. During the peg periods of 2000–2008 and 2010–2014, monetary policy was entirely constrained by the exchange-rate anchor: the NBU had no independent interest rate policy, and the "impossible trinity" was binding — with open capital flows and a fixed rate, domestic monetary policy had no degrees of freedom. The wartime fixed-rate regime introduced on 24 February 2022 likewise involved a deliberate suspension of exchange-rate flexibility in favour of macro-financial stability under extreme conditions.

The crisis episodes of 2008–2009 and 2014–2015 involved sharp depreciations, but these reflected forced adjustments rather than the deliberate exercise of monetary sovereignty. The exchange rate was not used as a policy instrument but as a safety valve when the peg became indefensible.

The clearest period of genuine monetary sovereignty was **January 2016 – February 2022**, when the NBU operated under a credible inflation-targeting framework with a floating exchange rate and an independent policy rate. Inflation fell from over 40% (2015) to around 5% (2019), and the NBU demonstrated capacity to adjust the policy rate in response to domestic conditions rather than external anchors.

For Part B, this implies that Euro Area membership cannot be treated as a constant treatment over the whole sample. The treatment effect is **near zero during peg periods** (Ukraine had already surrendered monetary autonomy), **moderate during the IT period** (Ukraine had partial but genuine sovereignty), and **maximal during the devaluation episodes** (when the exchange rate was the primary adjustment mechanism). This time-varying treatment intensity is built into the Blanchard-Quah counterfactual through regime-dependent weights on the demand shock replacement.
