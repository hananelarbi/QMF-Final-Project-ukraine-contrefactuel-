# Part A — Ukraine’s Monetary Regime: A Chronological Analysis (2000–2025)

## 1. Summary Table

| Period | Approx. UAH/USD rate | De facto regime assessment | Key events |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 2000–Mar. 2008 | ~5.00–5.06 | Stabilized arrangement (de facto USD peg) | The NBU maintained a tight exchange-rate band, using the exchange rate as a nominal anchor during a period of strong growth. Monetary policy was therefore closely tied to exchange-rate stability. |
| Apr. 2008–2009 | 4.85 → 7.88 | Managed arrangement with sharp devaluation | The global financial crisis triggered deposit withdrawals, capital outflows, and a sharp depreciation of the hryvnia. Significant capital controls were introduced from October 2008. |
| 2010–Jan. 2014 | ~7.99–8.15 | Stabilized arrangement (re-peg to USD) | The NBU re-established a stable exchange rate around 8 UAH/USD, supported by heavy FX intervention. From late 2012, reserves declined and FX restrictions were tightened again, including mandatory surrender requirements. |
| Feb. 2014–2015 | 8 → ~30 | Floating (crisis-driven adjustment) | Following the Maidan revolution, the annexation of Crimea, and the Donbas conflict, the peg collapsed and the hryvnia depreciated sharply. Strict capital controls were introduced in early 2015, including Decree No. 124 of 24 February 2015. |
| 2016–23 Feb. 2022 | ~25–28 (fluctuating) | Managed float with inflation targeting | The NBU adopted inflation targeting and shifted toward a more flexible exchange rate. The official disinflation path targeted 12% ±3 pp for end-2016, 8% ±2 pp for end-2017, 6% ±2 pp for end-2018, before converging toward 5%. This period shows the clearest behavioral shift toward a modern monetary framework. |
| 24 Feb. 2022–2 Oct. 2023 | 29.25 → 36.57 | Stabilized arrangement (wartime peg) | After the full-scale Russian invasion, the NBU fixed the exchange rate at 29.25 UAH/USD, imposed strict capital controls, and suspended much of the FX market. On 21 July 2022, the official rate was adjusted to 36.57 UAH/USD. |
| 3 Oct. 2023–2025 | ~36.6 → ~42 | Managed float (gradual normalization) | The NBU moved to “managed flexibility” from 3 October 2023, allowing the exchange rate to be increasingly market-determined while continuing to manage FX pressures and gradually ease wartime restrictions. |

## 2. Short Argument: When Did Ukraine Have Genuine Monetary Sovereignty?

Ukraine’s monetary history shows that effective monetary sovereignty varied substantially over time and was most clearly present during 2016–23 February 2022. During the peg periods of 2000–2008 and 2010–2014, monetary policy was heavily constrained by the exchange-rate anchor, which limited the NBU’s ability to respond independently to domestic shocks. The wartime fixed-rate regime introduced on 24 February 2022 likewise involved a deliberate suspension of exchange-rate flexibility in favor of macro-financial stability. By contrast, the crisis episodes of 2008–2009 and 2014–2015 involved sharp depreciations, but these reflected forced adjustments rather than the operation of a credible and autonomous monetary framework. The clearest break came with the transition to inflation targeting in 2015–2016: the NBU increasingly relied on the policy rate as its main instrument, inflation fell markedly, and exchange-rate flexibility increased. For Part B, this implies that Euro Area membership cannot be treated as a constant shock over the whole sample. The treatment effect should be relatively small during peg periods, when Ukraine had already surrendered much of its effective monetary autonomy, and stronger during crisis and inflation-targeting periods, when exchange-rate flexibility and domestic monetary policy mattered much more.
